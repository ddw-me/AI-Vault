# Time Management and Using This Vault

This vault is a practical note-taking and review system. It is meant to help you capture commitments, decide what matters next, protect focused time, and keep unfinished work from disappearing.

The approach is informed by Thomas A. Limoncelli's book [Time Management for System Administrators](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/time-management-for/0596007833/), published by O'Reilly. The book is written for system administrators, but its central ideas apply to anyone who needs a reliable way to manage requests, tasks, interruptions, and longer-running work. This note summarizes those ideas and adapts them to this vault; it is not a replacement for the book.

The immediate value of this system is practical: it helps you manage today's time, remember commitments, and keep tasks from disappearing. Its deeper value accumulates over time. The notes become durable context that you can search and review, and that an AI assistant with access to them can help curate, summarize, and use to guide you. The system is useful on the first day, but it becomes more valuable as the record develops.

## Why this belongs in the vault

The vault is not just a place to store notes. It is an external memory and a lightweight operating system for personal work:

- capture requests, ideas, decisions, and reminders where they can be found again;
- turn useful captures into a next action, a project, a calendar commitment, or reference material;
- plan a manageable day instead of relying on memory or reacting to every interruption;
- review unfinished work regularly so it is deliberately continued, rescheduled, delegated, or dropped.

The system should reduce mental overhead, not become another project to maintain. Start with daily notes and add more structure only when it solves a real problem.

For a worked example of how the notes, routines, Git, and optional AI assistance fit together, see [Time Management in Practice](./Time%20Management%20in%20Practice.md).

## Core principles

These are the principles behind the workflow:

1. **Use one trusted place for working information.** Keep tasks, notes, decisions, and follow-ups in the vault instead of scattering them across loose files, messages, and memory. A calendar may remain the source of truth for appointments; the daily note records the commitments that affect the day.
2. **Do not trust memory for commitments.** If something matters, write it down when it arrives. The goal is not to remember every detail; it is to remember where the detail is recorded.
3. **Conserve attention for important work.** Reduce unnecessary context switching, make the next action clear, and protect blocks of focused time when possible.
4. **Use routines instead of repeated decisions.** A short morning plan, continuous capture, an end-of-day cleanup, and weekly and monthly reviews create a dependable rhythm.
5. **Work in manageable pieces.** Break vague or oversized tasks into a next action that fits the available time and energy.
6. **Make unfinished work explicit.** At the end of a day or review period, every open item should have a deliberate disposition. It should not simply vanish from view.
7. **Keep the system visible and adaptable.** The vault should help a person see what is happening and what needs attention. Simplify it when the structure creates more work than value.
8. **Reduce friction over time.** When work is repeatedly difficult to start, resume, or complete, improve the path rather than relying only on more effort. Clarify the next action, make the task smaller, create a reusable template, remove an unnecessary step, delegate it, or consider automation when the recurring effort justifies its upkeep.

## Delegate, Record, or Do

When a request or task arrives, make an explicit choice instead of saying, “I will remember that.” The three choices are:

### Delegate

Give the work to the person or system that should handle it. Record who owns it, what outcome is expected, and when a follow-up is needed. Delegating does not mean forgetting; it means keeping enough information to verify that the handoff is complete.

### Record

Write it down when it is not urgent, when someone else owns it, or when it cannot be done immediately. Record the next action, relevant context, and any date or condition that matters. Put it in a place where it will be seen again.

In this vault, that usually means:

- a new thought, request, or reminder goes into the current `Daily Notes/` file;
- work that needs multiple steps or will last beyond the day moves into `Active Projects/`;
- a commitment that belongs to a regular review is surfaced during the weekly or monthly workflow;
- stable knowledge belongs in a useful reference or cheatsheet rather than being left only in a daily note.

### Do

Do it now when it is genuinely urgent, small enough to complete, and you are the right person to do it. Mark the result in the daily note or project note so the outcome is visible.

“Do” should not become a reason to interrupt every important task. If the request is not urgent, record it and return to the plan.

## The cycle

The book's cycle is a repeatable loop for turning commitments into completed work. This vault provides the notes and review points for that loop.

1. **Start with commitments.** Check the calendar, known deadlines, active projects, follow-ups, and the previous daily note.
2. **Create today's working list.** Gather carry-forwards and new items into today's daily note. Capture enough context that the item can be understood later.
3. **Choose what fits.** Select the Daily Frog and a small set of priorities. Estimate the available time and break large items into smaller next actions. Do not plan more work than the day can reasonably hold.
4. **Work the plan.** Make progress, check off completed items, and capture new requests or discoveries as they arrive. Use Delegate, Record, or Do when interruptions appear.
5. **Manage the open items.** At the end of the day, review incomplete work. Carry it forward with a reason and next action, move it to the appropriate project, delegate it, reschedule it, or decide that it is no longer needed.
6. **Review and repeat.** Use the weekly review to look across days and projects, and the monthly review to look at longer arcs and goals. The next daily note starts from managed information, not from a blank page or an attempt to reconstruct the past from memory.

## The Daily Frog

The Daily Frog is a memorable name for the most important useful action for the day. It is not a demand to finish an entire project, and it is not a measure of personal worth. Choose a concrete next step that would make the day meaningfully better if completed.

If the Frog keeps carrying forward, treat that as information. The item may be too large, poorly defined, blocked, lower priority than it appeared, or no longer necessary. Break it down, change the plan, or deliberately remove it.

## Use friction as information

The goal is not perfect compliance with the workflow. It is to make useful work easier to start, resume, and complete. When a task keeps being postponed or a note repeatedly takes too much effort to maintain, ask what is creating the resistance. The problem may be an unclear next action, too much scope, missing information, a repeated manual step, or a commitment that no longer matters.

Choose the smallest useful improvement. Clarify the next action, split the task, add a template, change where the information is recorded, remove an unnecessary step, or delegate the work. If the same friction recurs, consider a more durable process change or automation. A delay is information about the system or the situation, not a reason to shame yourself.

## A lightweight daily routine

### Start of day

- Open the previous daily note and review incomplete items.
- Check the calendar and other fixed commitments.
- Decide what should carry forward and what should be left behind.
- Choose one Daily Frog and a small number of realistic priorities.

### During the day

- Capture requests, decisions, and useful observations in the daily note.
- Use Delegate, Record, or Do for new work.
- Keep project work in the relevant project note while recording important progress in the daily note.
- Protect focused time by making interruptions visible and returning to the next action.

### End of day

- Mark completed work and record meaningful outcomes.
- Review every unfinished item.
- Carry forward only what still matters, with a next action or reason.
- Leave a short starting point for tomorrow.

## Reviews keep the cycle healthy

Daily notes handle immediate attention. The [Weekly Review](../000%20Workflows/Weekly%20Review.md) helps identify commitments that are drifting, projects that need a next step, and patterns in how time and attention were used. The [Monthly Review](../000%20Workflows/Monthly%20Review.md) provides a longer view of goals, themes, and changes in direction.

The reviews are not additional reporting for its own sake. Their purpose is to make sure the system remains trustworthy and that important work does not get buried in daily detail.
